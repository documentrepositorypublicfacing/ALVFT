The Alabama State Department of Education (SDE) invites you to participate in our popular monthly Virtual Field Trips. Alabama has been a trailblazer using technology for the purpose of advancing education. Alabama Connecting Classrooms, Educators, and Students Statewide (ACCESS), Alabama Learning Exchange (ALEX), Alabama Technology in Motion (ATiM), and e-Learning for Educators are all strong tools used for the advancement of digital learning, in addition to the expansive ways technology is used in the classroom.

Culture, Contact and Conflict
Jake Smith, Education Curator, Alabama Department of Archives & History
Grades 3–6
October 13 — 9:00–10:00 AM October 13 — 10:15–11:15 AM October 22 — 9:00–10:00 AM

Building off of last month’s exploration of the First Peoples of this land, students will learn how these First Peoples managed the major changes brought by contact with European settlers and the creation of the United States of America. Students will get to see a number of artifacts related to this time period.

Alabama Standards
  • SS24.3.8b — Describe key characteristics of different tribes in Alabama in the Mississippian Period, contrasting social customs, political organization, and religious practices.
  • SS24.3.9b — Describe key parts of Hernando de Soto’s expedition through Alabama and explain its effects on Alabama’s Indigenous peoples.
  • SS24.4.1 — Describe cooperation and conflict between the English, French, and Spanish colonists and Indigenous people over the fur trade, slave trade, and land claims.
  • SS24.4.8a — Summarize how the Federal Road increased access to Creek Territory for settlers and enslaved persons, and explain how their arrival affected Native Americans.
Setting Up the Civil War, Pt. 1: Three Kinds of Southerners
Christian Wysmulek, Education Curator, Alabama Department of Archives & History
Grades 4–6 & 9–12
October 20 — 9:00–10:00 AM (Grades 4–6) October 20 — 10:15–11:15 AM (Grades 4–6) October 22 — 10:15–11:15 AM (Grades 9–12)

Why was the Civil War fought mostly here in the South? What were the goals of each army and social group? Join the Alabama Archives in exploring these questions and more as we consider events that led to the war and Alabama’s connections to them through the voices of three kinds of Southerners: “fire-eater” William Lowndes Yancey, author and self-emancipated slave Henry Goings, and the schoolteacher turned Unionist leader Christopher Sheats.

Alabama Standards
  • SS24.4.11a — Locate states admitted to the United States between 1812 and 1860 and categorize these states as either “free” or “slave” states.
  • SS24.4.13 — Describe how slavery contributed to sectional disagreements, divisions over states’ rights, and economic disputes resulting in the Civil War.
  • SS24.4.13d — Summarize Alabama’s role in the formation of the Confederate States of America, including the pivotal events that took place in Montgomery and the division over secession in the northern part of the state.
  • SS24.4.14 — Evaluate how key figures contributed to events that shaped the United States’ victory in the Civil War.
  • SS24.6.12 — Explain how forms of governments established prior to the founding of the United States influenced the formation of the new nation’s system of government.
  • SS24.US1.11 — Analyze how government policies and events related to slavery led to increased sectionalism throughout the United States prior to the Civil War.
  • SS24.US1.12 — Explain the progression of the Civil War, analyzing the events and the decisions by key figures that led to United States victory and the abolition of slavery.
American Women Who Changed History
Keller Johnson-Thompson, President, Helen Keller Education Foundation
Grades 1–6
October 21 — 9:00–10:00 AM

American history is filled with remarkable women whose courage, vision, intelligence, and determination opened doors, challenged injustice, advanced education, strengthened communities, and changed the course of our nation. This presentation gives teachers a compelling way to introduce students to women whose stories deserve to be remembered — not as distant names in a textbook, but as real people who used their voices, talents, and convictions to make a lasting difference.

Through these inspiring examples, students will discover that history is shaped by individuals who are willing to lead, serve, question, create, and persevere. Teachers will find this program especially meaningful because it connects character, citizenship, leadership, and resilience in a way that encourages students to see their own potential to influence the world around them.

A Journey Into the Life of Helen Keller
Keller Johnson-Thompson, great-grandniece of Helen Keller and President of the Helen Keller Education Foundation
Grades 3–12
October 21 — 10:15–11:15 AM October 28 — 9:00–10:00 AM

Keller Johnson-Thompson, great-grandniece of Helen Keller and President of the Helen Keller Education Foundation, offers teachers a meaningful and engaging way to bring Helen Keller’s story to life for students. This presentation takes students on a memorable journey through the remarkable life of Helen Keller, America’s First Lady of Courage — from her childhood in Tuscumbia, Alabama, to her travels and influence around the world.

More than a history lesson, this program helps students understand perseverance, empathy, courage, and the importance of strong character. By connecting Helen Keller’s inspiring example to values students can use in their own lives, this presentation supports classroom learning while encouraging students to believe in their ability to make a positive difference in the world.

Make Your Voice Count
The National Archives Education Team, Museum Operations Division
Grades 3–5
October 26 — 9:30–10:15 AM

The Bill of Rights, the first 10 amendments to the Constitution, contains many of the nation’s most valued freedoms. Using the Bill of Rights as the starting point, this program invites students to explore the National Archives’ new permanent exhibit, The American Story, to discover how Americans have exercised their First Amendment rights to make their voices count. Students will apply primary source analysis skills to answer the following questions:

  • How have Americans exercised their First Amendment rights to bring about change in their communities?
  • Why should First Amendment rights matter to us today?
Eye Spy Spooky, Slimy Sea Monsters
Kyle Halstead, Discovery Hall Programs Educator
All Grades
October 27 — 9:00–10:00 AM (K–5 focus) October 27 — 10:15–11:15 AM (Middle & High School focus)

Do you know what goes bump in the eternal night of the midnight zone? Have you ever seen something wash up on the beach that’s made you go “Jeepers?!” Join Discovery Hall Programs as we slither into some spooky science meant to give you goosefish-bumps just in time for Halloween. In this presentation, we will discuss sea animals with some seriously spooky, slimy, or monstrous get-ups, pull some tricks, and give y’all a treat in getting to see all sorts of things up close.

The 9:00 AM session is geared toward younger students (K–5); the 10:15 AM session is more suitable for middle and high school students. Addresses the disciplinary core ideas of molecules to organisms (structures and processes), unity and diversity, and ecosystems (interactions, energy, and dynamics).

Amazing African Americans from Alabama, 2nd Edition
Keller Johnson-Thompson, President, Helen Keller Education Foundation
Grades 5–12
October 28 — 10:15–11:15 AM

Alabama’s history is filled with extraordinary African Americans whose courage, creativity, leadership, and determination helped shape our state, our nation, and the world. This field trip gives teachers a powerful opportunity to introduce students to inspiring real-life role models who prove that history is not just something to read about; it is something students can connect with, question, and carry forward. Through these remarkable stories, students will discover examples of resilience, achievement, and service that can spark classroom conversations, build pride in Alabama’s rich heritage, and encourage every student to imagine the difference they too can make.
